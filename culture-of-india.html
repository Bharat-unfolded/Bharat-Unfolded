India: A civilisation
of many stories

From the cities of the Indus Valley to a modern democratic republic, India’s story is shaped by many languages, faiths, landscapes, communities, ideas and creative traditions.

India is not one single culture or one uniform way of life. It is a vast, multilingual and multi-religious country where regional identities thrive alongside shared national institutions. Its heritage can be seen in ancient cities, living temples, mosques, churches, gurdwaras, monasteries, music, dance, food, crafts, literature and everyday community life.

A short history of India

India’s past is long and varied. Dates for early periods are approximate, and different regions often developed at the same time under different rulers and traditions.

c. 2600–1900 BCE

Indus Valley Civilisation

Planned cities such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro had organised streets, drainage, craft production and long-distance trade. Dholavira and other sites show the civilisation’s wide reach.

2nd millennium BCE onward

Vedic and later Vedic traditions

Vedic hymns and ritual traditions developed and changed over centuries. Sanskrit literature, philosophical debate and evolving social institutions became important parts of the subcontinent’s history.

c. 600–300 BCE

Mahajanapadas and new ideas

Large states and republic-like gana-sanghas emerged. Magadha grew powerful. Buddhism and Jainism developed in this period, alongside many other schools of thought.

c. 322–185 BCE

Mauryan Empire

Chandragupta Maurya established a large empire with Pataliputra as a major capital. Ashoka’s inscriptions and support for dhamma and Buddhism remain important historical records.

c. 200 BCE–300 CE

Regional kingdoms and trade

Indo-Greek, Shaka, Satavahana, Kushan, Chera, Chola and Pandya powers, among others, shaped trade, sculpture, coins, literature and connections across Asia and the Indian Ocean.

c. 4th–6th centuries CE

Gupta age and classical learning

Gupta rulers supported Sanskrit literature, temple art and scholarship. Mathematics, astronomy, metallurgy and philosophy developed across several centres and traditions.

c. 7th–12th centuries

Temples, kingdoms and learning

Major regional powers included the Pallavas, Chalukyas, Rashtrakutas, Palas, Pratiharas, Cholas and many others. Nalanda and Vikramashila became renowned centres of Buddhist learning.

1206–1526

Delhi Sultanate and regional sultanates

Several dynasties ruled from Delhi while regional kingdoms and sultanates flourished elsewhere. Indo-Islamic architecture, Persianate court culture, Sufi traditions and Bhakti movements shaped society.

1526–18th century

Mughal and other early-modern powers

The Mughal Empire expanded across much of northern India, while the Deccan sultanates, Rajput states, Ahom kingdom, Marathas, Sikhs and other powers shaped regional histories.

18th–19th centuries

Company rule and colonial India

The East India Company expanded its control after battles including Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764). The 1857 uprising became a major turning point; colonial rule reshaped land, economy and institutions.

1885–1947

Freedom movement

Political organisations, reform movements, workers, peasants, revolutionaries and leaders across regions challenged colonial rule. Gandhi’s mass movements, Subhas Chandra Bose, Bhagat Singh and countless local organisers formed part of this struggle.

1947–1950

Independence and the Republic

India became independent on 15 August 1947 amid Partition and mass displacement. The Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950, establishing India as a republic.

1950–today

Modern India

India built democratic institutions, expanded education and industry, reorganised states, developed a space programme and a major technology sector, and continued to debate questions of equality, development and diversity.

Population and people

India is the world’s most populous country. The UN estimated its 2025 population at about 1.464 billion. This is a statistical estimate; it should not be confused with a fresh national census. The 2011 Census counted about 1.21 billion residents.

Many communities

India includes thousands of communities and regional identities, including Adivasi and other Indigenous communities, linguistic groups, pastoral communities, coastal communities and urban populations.

Rural and urban life

Village agriculture, small towns, industrial centres and large metropolitan regions all contribute to the country’s social and economic life. Migration connects regions and generations.

Changing society

Education, public health, technology, transport, migration and changing work patterns continue to influence family life, opportunities and social relationships.

Population size, age structure, literacy, employment and household conditions vary widely between states and districts. For exact district-level comparisons, consult official Census tables rather than treating one national figure as the whole picture.

Languages of India

The Constitution lists 22 languages in its Eighth Schedule: Assamese, Bengali, Bodo, Dogri, Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada, Kashmiri, Konkani, Maithili, Malayalam, Manipuri (Meitei), Marathi, Nepali, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Santali, Sindhi, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu.

The 2011 Census language tables grouped returns into 121 languages. India also has many additional mother tongues, dialects and smaller languages. Hindi and English are used for Union government purposes under the Constitution and laws; states may adopt their own official languages.

Major language families include Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, Austroasiatic and Tibeto-Burman. Language boundaries do not always match state borders, and many people use more than one language in daily life.

Religions and philosophies

India is home to several major religions and a wide range of local, tribal and philosophical traditions. Hinduism, Islam, Christianity, Sikhism, Buddhism and Jainism have large and long-established communities. Jewish, Zoroastrian and Baháʼí communities, as well as many Indigenous and local traditions, are also part of India’s story.

Hindu, Buddhist, Jain and Sikh traditions all have deep historical roots in the subcontinent. Islam arrived through multiple routes including trade, migration and political rule; Christianity has ancient communities in parts of the south and later histories elsewhere. Religious practice differs greatly by region and community.

India’s Constitution protects freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to constitutional limits. Respect for different beliefs is essential when describing the country’s culture.

Festivals and celebrations

Across many regions

Diwali, Holi, Eid, Christmas, Gurpurab, Buddha Purnima, Mahavir Jayanti, Navratri and Dussehra are observed by communities in different ways.

Harvest and seasonal festivals

Pongal, Makar Sankranti, Baisakhi, Onam, Bihu, Nuakhai and Lohri mark harvests, seasonal change and community life.

Regional celebrations

Durga Puja in Bengal, Ganesh Chaturthi in Maharashtra, Rath Yatra in Odisha, Chhath in Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh, and Hornbill Festival in Nagaland are examples of local traditions.

Dates, rituals and food customs can differ between communities and calendars. Festivals are living practices, not identical events everywhere.

Dance, music, crafts and performing arts

Classical dance traditions

Recognised classical dance forms include Bharatanatyam, Kathak, Kathakali, Kuchipudi, Odissi, Manipuri, Mohiniyattam and Sattriya. Each has its own movement vocabulary, music, costume and history. India also has hundreds of folk and community dance traditions, such as Garba, Ghoomar, Bhangra, Bihu, Chhau, Lavani and Yakshagana performance traditions.

Music

Hindustani and Carnatic classical music are two major classical systems. Folk, devotional, Sufi, tribal, regional and contemporary popular music add many more sounds. Instruments include sitar, sarod, tabla, veena, mridangam, shehnai, bansuri, dhol and many regional instruments.

Visual arts and crafts

India’s art includes Madhubani and Mithila painting, Warli, Gond, Pattachitra, Phad, Kalamkari, miniature painting, block printing, weaving, embroidery, pottery, metalwork, wood carving, stone carving and contemporary art. Many crafts are sustained by artisan families and local cooperatives.

Indian food: a regional map of flavours

Indian cuisine is not one menu. Climate, crops, trade, religion, migration and local history shape ingredients and cooking styles. Rice, wheat, millets, pulses, vegetables, dairy, fish, meat, spices and fermented foods appear in different combinations.

North India

Roti, paratha, dal, chole, kebabs, rajma, sarson ka saag and a wide range of breads and dairy-based dishes.

South India

Idli, dosa, sambar, rasam, rice meals, appam, puttu, coconut-based curries and many coastal seafood dishes.

East India

Rice, fish, mustard flavours, sweets such as rasgulla and sandesh, litti-chokha in Bihar, and pitha traditions across the region.

West India

Dhokla, thepla, undhiyu, dal-baati-churma, Goan fish curries, Malvani food and coastal coconut dishes.

Central India

Poha, jalebi, bhutte ka kees, tribal forest foods, millet dishes and regional thalis.

North-East India

Rice, bamboo shoot, herbs, fermented foods, smoked meats and fish, leafy greens and distinct community cuisines.

Dietary customs vary within every region and household. No single dish or food rule represents all Indians.

Regional cultures across India

North

Himalayan, Punjabi, Kashmiri, Himachali, Uttarakhandi, Rajasthani and Uttar Pradesh traditions, with varied languages, crafts, music and food.

South

Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam and Tulu cultures, temple architecture, classical arts, coastal traditions and diverse literary histories.

East

Bengali, Odia, Bihari, Jharkhandi and other traditions shaped by rivers, forests, literature, crafts, festivals and farming.

West

Gujarati, Marathi, Goan and other western cultures, with maritime histories, trading cities, folk theatre, textiles and food.

North-East

Eight states with many distinct Indigenous communities, languages, weaving traditions, music, festivals and ecological knowledge.

Islands and coasts

Andaman and Nicobar Islands and Lakshadweep have distinctive island, maritime and Indigenous histories and cultures.

Architecture, monuments and heritage

India’s built heritage includes Indus cities, Buddhist stupas and monasteries, rock-cut caves, Hindu and Jain temples, mosques, dargahs, churches, gurdwaras, forts, palaces, stepwells, colonial-era buildings and modern architecture. Styles differ across time and place, shaped by local materials, patrons, artisans and exchange between communities.

UNESCO’s World Heritage List includes cultural, natural and mixed properties in India. Sites include the Taj Mahal, Ajanta and Ellora Caves, Hampi, the Sun Temple at Konark, the Mahabodhi Temple Complex at Bodh Gaya, the mountain railways and several natural parks. UNESCO listings change as new sites are inscribed; check the official list for the current total.

Literature, cinema and contemporary culture

India’s literary traditions range from the Vedas, epics, Sangam poetry, Buddhist and Jain texts, Bhakti and Sufi poetry to modern writing in many languages. Theatre, regional cinema, Bollywood, documentary film, television, digital creators, sports and contemporary design are active parts of cultural life today.
